# Peer Reviews: EABench — A Configurable End-to-End Platform for Evaluating Enterprise LLM Agents

---

## Review 1

**Reviewer:** Reviewer 1 (Systems / Benchmarking track)
**Recommendation:** Major Revision
**Confidence:** High

---

### Summary

This paper introduces EABench, an end-to-end platform for evaluating LLM-based enterprise agents. The central claim is that existing benchmarks are inadequate for enterprise evaluation because they fix datasets, hard-code metrics, or ignore access-controlled, cross-modal organizational structure. EABench addresses this through configurable tenant generation (synthetic organizational corpora), a YAML-driven agent runtime, automatic evaluation-dataset generation (three query types), an LLM-as-judge harness, and an interactive Streamlit debugging interface. The empirical evaluation covers three agent configurations (ReAct-v1, ReAct-v2, Researcher) across three synthetic tenant organizations. The paper is well-organized and the platform design is technically coherent, but several significant methodological and validity concerns need to be addressed before acceptance.

---

### Strengths

1. **Well-motivated problem.** The argument that enterprise evaluation requires co-design of data, runtime, and evaluation harness is genuinely compelling, and the paper articulates it clearly. The summary of competitor limitations (Table [comparison]) is fair and informative.

2. **Principled configurability.** Exposing six runtime dimensions (model, embedding, architecture, system prompt, tools, sandbox) via a single YAML file is a clean engineering contribution. The research questions each dimension enables (Table on runtime dimensions) are concrete and useful.

3. **Decomposed evaluation metrics.** Separating assertion satisfaction, tool-search relevance, and response-citation quality into three independent judge calls captures distinct failure modes and is a more informative metric design than a single composite score. The empirical finding that retrieval precision and citation formatting are largely independent failure modes (Section 5.3 discussion) is an interesting and actionable result.

4. **Scenario-level analysis.** Connecting graph-theoretic properties of simulated networks (density, diameter, burstiness) to observed agent behavior is a structurally sound framing that gives empirical findings a meaningful interpretation beyond raw score comparisons.

---

### Critical Concerns

**C1. Circular validity — the central methodological risk (Critical).**
EABench generates assertions using GPT-4o, evaluates agents using GPT-4o (judge), and two of three evaluated agents use GPT-4o (runtime). The paper acknowledges this circularity concern in Section 5.4 ("Cross-Model Evaluation"), but describes the cross-model evaluation protocol only as a framework design goal rather than a realized experiment. No results are reported using a different judge or a different generator. Given that all three "independent" components share the same model family, the reported scores may inflate GPT-4o-family agents and the self-preference bias the paper warns against may already be present in the main results table. At minimum, the paper should report at least one cross-model cell (e.g., tenant generated by one model family, judged by another) to demonstrate that the ranking of architectures is not an artifact of generator–judge alignment.

**C2. Assertion quality is unvalidated (Critical).**
The entire evaluation rests on the quality of automatically generated assertions. The paper describes how assertions are generated (from the same context used to formulate the query) but provides no evidence that these assertions are (i) factually grounded in the generated corpus, (ii) non-trivially answerable, (iii) internally consistent across cases, or (iv) at the difficulty level claimed. A human validation study on even a sample of 50–100 assertions per query type, reporting inter-annotator agreement and false-positive rates (assertions that the correct answer does not technically need to satisfy), is necessary to establish the validity of the primary metric. Without this, the assertion score is an unvalidated proxy.

**C3. Confounded agent comparison (Critical).**
Table 5 (agent configurations) reveals that the three agents differ on three axes simultaneously: architecture (ReAct vs. Researcher), model size (GPT-4o vs. GPT-4o-mini), and prompt verbosity/temperature. The paper attributes Researcher's planning advantage to "the separation of planning from execution" (Section 5.3), but Researcher also uses a *smaller* model (GPT-4o-mini) with *deterministic decoding* (T=0.0). If Researcher's broader retrieval footprint is what drives its pass-rate advantage, that advantage may derive from the configuration of 4.85 average tool calls — not from the planning stage per se. Analogously, the ReAct-v1 vs. ReAct-v2 comparison is confounded between prompt verbosity and temperature (0.7 vs. 0.5). The paper's causal claims (e.g., "The planning stage provides a measurable advantage") require controlled ablations where exactly one variable is changed at a time.

**C4. Evaluation reproducibility is limited by closed infrastructure (Important).**
All experiments use Azure OpenAI (GPT-4o, text-embedding-ada-002). These are commercial APIs whose model weights, versioning, and internal behavior may change without notice. Because the paper does not report the specific model deployment version or snapshot timestamp, exact replication is impossible. For a paper making reproducibility a first-class claim, this is a notable gap. The paper should report API snapshot dates and commit to preserving result artifacts (e.g., full JSON outputs per case) in a public repository.

**C5. Statistical analysis is absent (Important).**
The paper presents pass rates and metric averages across 162–201 cases per agent-tenant cell with no confidence intervals, standard errors, or significance tests. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test is mentioned as a planned analysis in Section 3.5, but no such test appears in the results. Without uncertainty quantification, the meaningfulness of small score differences — e.g., 0.414 (ReAct-v1 Bertrand) vs. 0.432 (Researcher Bertrand), a gap of 1.8 percentage points — cannot be assessed. The 52% relative improvement claim for Cambford (0.228 → 0.346) sounds compelling as a relative number, but a confidence interval around each estimate is necessary.

**C6. Platform realism claims are unverified (Important).**
The paper makes repeated realism claims: "realistic organizational archetypes," "hub individuals," "realistic enterprise communication." These are supported by graph-theoretic analysis (degree distributions, burstiness, reciprocity) but never validated against actual organizational corpora. Do the generated tenants' statistical properties match empirical distributions from real enterprise email corpora (e.g., Enron, Avocado)? The absence of such a comparison significantly weakens the claim that EABench provides "realistic operational constraints."

---

### Minor Concerns

**M1. Query-type classification is post-hoc and heuristic.**
The paper reports query-type breakdowns (Section 5.6) using a classification heuristic (entity count ≤ 1 → fact-check; regex on verb → report; else → multi-hop). This heuristic is described in detail in the analysis code but not in the paper text, making it invisible to readers. The classification scheme should be formally stated, and its agreement with ground-truth query-type labels (if available) reported.

**M2. Pass rate definition is strict to a fault.**
A case is marked "passed" only when assertion score is exactly 1.0 (all assertions satisfied). This binary threshold with zero tolerance is unusually strict; partial credit is standard in most evaluation frameworks. With this definition, a response satisfying 95% of assertions scores identically to one satisfying 0%, which may not reflect enterprise utility. The paper should either justify this threshold decision theoretically or report results for a softened threshold as a sensitivity analysis.

**M3. Missing reference.**
The citation key `goh2008burstiness` is flagged as missing in the bibliography. The burstiness parameter $B$ is used in Tables 6 and 7, but the defining citation is absent. This must be fixed.

**M4. Manuscript formatting artifact.**
All methodology and abstract text is typeset in blue (`\textcolor{blue}{...}`), which is presumably a draft-stage artifact. These coloring commands must be removed before submission.

**M5. Related-work overlap.**
WorkArena is cited twice in the Related Work section: once in the Agent Benchmarks subsection and once in the Information Worker Benchmarks paragraph with different descriptions. These should be merged.

**M6. "GAIA-2" citation.**
The paper cites `\cite{gaia2}` but the bibliography entry for GAIA-2 is not visible in the provided bibliography. If this is a different work from `mialon2023gaia` (GAIA), it needs a distinct entry.

---

### Recommendations

The paper makes a genuine and useful contribution to the enterprise AI benchmarking literature, but its current form relies too heavily on self-reported design quality without adequate empirical validation of the core measurement pipeline. The following changes are required:

1. Report at least one cross-model evaluation cell demonstrating generator–judge independence.
2. Provide human validation of assertion quality for a sample of cases per query type.
3. Add standard errors or confidence intervals to all reported metrics.
4. Decouple the agent comparison by isolating architectural and prompt variables.
5. Fix the missing `goh2008burstiness` reference and the blue-text formatting artifact.

With these changes, the paper would be a strong contribution.

## Review 2

**Reviewer:** Reviewer 2 (NLP / LLM Evaluation track)
**Recommendation:** Major Revision
**Confidence:** High

---

### Summary

EABench presents a configurable platform for enterprise LLM agent evaluation, combining synthetic organizational corpus generation, a YAML-driven agent runtime, automatic evaluation-case synthesis, and an LLM-as-judge harness. The paper makes a reasonable case that enterprise agent evaluation is under-resourced in the literature, and the platform design is well-engineered. However, from a scientific methodology standpoint, several concerns about measurement validity, generalization, and claim calibration must be addressed.

---

### Strengths

1. **Thoughtful metrics decomposition.** The separation of assertion satisfaction, tool-search relevance, and response-citation quality operationalizes three qualitatively distinct capabilities. The insight that these failure modes are largely orthogonal (ReAct-v1 leads on tool-search relevance; Researcher leads on response-citation quality) is a genuine empirical contribution that would not surface from a single composite score.

2. **Interpretable scenario analysis.** The connection between network-theoretic properties (density, diameter, burstiness) and agent performance differences across tenants (e.g., how dense networks favor reactive agents, sparse networks favor planners) provides a satisfying mechanistic narrative for the empirical results.

3. **Explicit acknowledgment of limitations.** The conclusion names three specific limitations (English-only, dependence on capable foundation models, judge prompt scope) rather than relegating them to a brief disclaimer. This is commendable.

4. **YAML-driven configurability.** Representing agent configurations as version-controllable YAML files that include model selection, embedding, system prompt, tool set, and flow strategy in one artifact is a principled and practically useful design.

---

### Critical Concerns

**C1. LLM-as-judge validity is not established (Critical).**
The paper adapts the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm (citing MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena) but does not measure the agreement between GPT-4o's judgments and human annotations on EABench cases. LLM judges are known to exhibit length bias (preferring longer responses), position bias, and self-preference bias. For a benchmark whose primary purpose is to compare agent architectures, the claim that the judge reliably distinguishes performance levels requires at minimum an inter-rater agreement study between the LLM judge and human assessors on a representative sample of cases. The Researcher agent, which retrieves 3.5× more content and produces structurally longer responses with more citations, might be systematically favored by a judge that conflates citation volume with citation quality. The decomposed metrics partially guard against this, but without human validation, this risk cannot be dismissed.

**C2. Generalization from three tenants to enterprise evaluation is under-characterized (Critical).**
Three tenant organizations (one law firm, one university, one AI startup) are insufficient to establish the generalizability of empirical findings to "enterprise evaluation" broadly. The paper does not compute or report sensitivity of the agent rankings to tenant-specific characteristics (e.g., what would happen with a finance, healthcare, or manufacturing tenant?). The ranking of architectures could in principle reverse for tenants not represented in the current evaluation set, and the paper makes no argument for why the three chosen domains are representative of the enterprise distribution. At minimum, additional tenants or a characterization of the space of enterprise scenarios and where the three tenants sit within it would strengthen the claims.

**C3. The report query type is dominated by synthesis and likely confounded with length (Critical).**
Report queries require "long-form synthesis, temporal organization, and citation-grounded summarization." The Researcher agent, which retrieves 3.5× more artifacts, produces responses with higher grounded-citation counts (2.81 vs. 1.87) and higher response-citation scores. It is plausible that the judge rewards longer, more heavily cited responses independently of factual accuracy — and indeed the assertion score for Researcher (0.557) is the *lowest* of the three agents. The paper attributes this to "smaller model capacity and deterministic decoding" but does not test whether the citation-score advantage disappears when response length is controlled. A partial correlation between citation score and response length, controlling for assertion accuracy, would be informative.

**C4. The pass-rate metric interacts with assertion generation quality (Important).**
Pass rate is defined as the fraction of cases where assertion score = 1.0 (all assertions satisfied). Since assertions are generated automatically by GPT-4o with no human verification, a case can "pass" simply because the generated assertions are easy or tautologically linked to retrieved artifacts. The paper reports overall pass rates of 21–43%, which sounds plausible but cannot be interpreted without knowing the baseline pass rate of a trivial agent (e.g., one that simply retrieves the top-$k$ documents and summarizes them without any reasoning). Including a retrieval-only baseline would anchor the reported scores.

**C5. The temperature difference between agents conflates exploration and capability (Important).**
ReAct-v1 (T=0.7) vs. ReAct-v2 (T=0.5) differ in temperature by 0.2. The paper argues that T=0.7 enables "stochastic variation in search queries" that surfaces otherwise-missed documents. However, with only a single run per agent-case combination (which appears to be the experimental setup, though never explicitly stated), the temperature effect cannot be separated from run-to-run stochasticity. If ReAct-v1 was run once per case, some of its gains may reflect favorable random outcomes rather than systematic capability. A multi-run analysis with variance reporting would clarify this.

---

### Minor Concerns

**M1. Cost analysis is incomplete.**
The paper reports prompt token counts but not cost in USD, API call counts, or the token costs for judge evaluation (four separate LLM calls per case). For a platform paper whose cost-effectiveness is a central argument, a complete cost breakdown (generator + runtime + judge) per agent-tenant cell would enable practitioners to estimate deployment costs.

**M2. "Extreme configurability" overstates the contribution.**
The abstract claims EABench "exposes extreme configurability." In practice, the runtime supports two cognitive architectures (ReAct, Plan-and-Execute), one embedding model family (Ada-002 in experiments), and a fixed set of search tools. While the framework is designed for extensibility, and the YAML design is sound, the phrase "extreme configurability" is a marketing claim that is not supported by demonstrated breadth.

**M3. The query-type analysis (Section 5.6) lacks statistical grounding.**
The query-type breakdown (Figures for assertion_by_query_type, citation_by_query_type) presents bar charts with no error bars or significance annotations. Given the varying sample sizes per cell (fact-check: 239, multi-hop: 169, report: 117), the within-type variance is likely substantial and differences that appear visually large may not be statistically reliable.

**M4. "Needle-in-a-haystack" framing is potentially misleading.**
The paper uses "needle-in-a-haystack retrieval" to describe fact-check queries (single-entity retrieval). In the literature, needle-in-a-haystack specifically refers to long-context retrieval where context length is the primary challenge. EABench's search index limits each tool call to returning a bounded number of documents from a relatively small corpus (max ~550 emails, ~300 meetings, ~300 files per tenant), which is not the difficulty regime the phrase implies. Renaming this query type to "single-entity retrieval" or "precision retrieval" would be more precise.

**M5. Discussion of failure modes is qualitative.**
Section 5.5 identifies three failure modes (wrong tool choice, weak evidence retrieval, multi-hop chain loss, incorrect citation of correct artifact) but these are stated as hypotheses rather than measured taxonomy. A failure categorization study on a sample of failed cases, with each failure assigned to a category, would substantially strengthen the diagnostic claims.

**M6. Interactive debugging interface lacks evaluation.**
Section 3.6 describes the Streamlit debugging interface and argues it "supports debugging by diagnosis rather than by prompt trial-and-error alone." No evidence is provided that researchers actually use this interface more efficiently than alternative debugging approaches. A brief usability study or even a documented debugging session would support this claim.

---

### Recommendations

The paper's platform design is sound and the decomposed metric framework is a genuine contribution. The following changes are necessary for acceptance:

1. Report human-judge agreement on a sample of assertion evaluations to validate the LLM-judge pipeline.
2. Include a retrieval-only baseline agent to anchor the pass-rate scale.
3. Address the confounding between agent architecture, model size, and decoding temperature through controlled ablations.
4. Add error bars to all figures and significance tests to key comparisons.
5. Extend evaluation to at least one additional tenant domain to test generalization.
6. Rename "needle-in-a-haystack" to a more precise term.

---

## Review 3

**Reviewer:** Reviewer 3 (Applied AI / Enterprise Systems track)
**Recommendation:** Accept with Minor Revisions
**Confidence:** Medium-High

---

### Summary

EABench is an end-to-end platform for evaluating enterprise LLM agents. It contributes: (1) a configurable synthetic corpus generator that produces multi-modal organizational artifacts (emails, meetings, files, chats) based on a narrative scenario specification; (2) a YAML-driven agent runtime supporting ReAct and Plan-and-Execute strategies; (3) an automatic evaluation-dataset generator producing three query types with ground-truth assertions and entity lists; (4) an LLM-as-judge evaluation harness; and (5) an interactive Streamlit debugging interface. Experiments across three tenants and three agent configurations yield interpretable findings about the interaction between agent architecture, prompt engineering, and scenario properties. The work is well-motivated, clearly written, and the engineering design is principled. Some validity concerns are worth addressing, but the paper is close to publication-ready.

---

### Strengths

1. **Systems-level thinking is the right framing.** Treating enterprise agent evaluation as a co-design problem across data, runtime, and harness is conceptually correct. Prior work that fixes one of these while varying another produces findings that are difficult to interpret because performance depends on the interactions between them. EABench's integrated design addresses this directly.

2. **The scenario-network-behavior connection is novel.** Characterizing tenants using network graph properties (density, diameter, burstiness, reciprocity) and then showing how these properties predict agent behavior differences is an original analytical contribution. The observation that agents with planning benefits from sparse networks and that reactive agents are competitive on dense networks has actionable implications for deployment.

3. **The YAML configuration schema is a practical contribution.** The ability to version-control a complete agent specification — model, embedding, prompts, tools, strategy — in a single file that reproduces behavior without code changes is practically important for the research community. This is not merely a software convenience; it is a prerequisite for reproducible research.

4. **Cost-effectiveness analysis is honest.** The paper does not obscure the 5.2× token cost and 3.7× latency overhead of the Researcher agent. The identification of ReAct-v2 as a Pareto-suboptimal configuration is a useful finding for practitioners. The cost-quality frontier framing (Figure latency_vs_pass, Figure cost_frontier) is an appropriate representation for the applied audience.

5. **The decomposed citation metric catches an important failure mode.** Distinguishing between tool-search relevance (did the agent retrieve the right evidence?) and response-citation quality (did it cite that evidence correctly?) identifies a failure mode — correct retrieval without proper attribution — that would be invisible to a unified citation metric. This is a meaningful methodological contribution.

---

### Concerns

**C1. Human validation of assertions is missing (Important).**
The assertion-generation pipeline is fully automated, and assertions are generated by the same model family (GPT-4o) that judges them. Although the generation and evaluation prompts are distinct, the paper provides no human-validation study confirming that the generated assertions are (a) grounded in the tenant corpus, (b) nontrivially answerable, and (c) reflective of genuine enterprise information needs. Without this, the primary evaluation metric rests on an unverified foundation. A relatively small sample study (e.g., 3 annotators × 50 cases per query type × 2 tenants) reporting inter-annotator agreement and false-positive assertion rate would be sufficient.

**C2. The three-agent confounding limits causal claims (Important).**
The three evaluated agents differ simultaneously in architecture, model size (GPT-4o vs. GPT-4o-mini), system prompt length, temperature, and tool call budget. The Discussion in Section 5.5 names "the separation of planning from execution" as the architectural advantage of Researcher, but this cannot be isolated given that Researcher also uses a smaller, deterministic model. For the claims about architecture to be convincing, at least one clean ablation is needed: e.g., ReAct with GPT-4o-mini at T=0.0 and 5 max turns, to separate the effect of the planning stage from the effect of model/temperature choice.

**C3. Statistical significance is not reported (Important).**
Pass-rate differences as small as 0.018 (Bertrand, ReAct-v1 0.414 vs. Researcher 0.432) and as large as 0.118 (Cambford, Researcher 0.346 vs. ReAct-v2 0.228) are reported without confidence intervals. With 162 cases per cell and binary outcomes (pass/fail), a Wilson confidence interval is straightforward to compute (e.g., a 95% CI for pass rate 0.346 over 162 cases has half-width approximately ±0.073). These intervals would clarify which differences are reliable and which are within noise. Key aggregate claims ("52% relative pass-rate improvement on Cambford") should be accompanied by a significance test given the importance the paper places on this finding.

**C4. The missing `goh2008burstiness` reference must be fixed (Minor).**
BibTeX issues two database-level warnings and one `not found` for `goh2008burstiness`. The burstiness parameter $B$ is central to the temporal analysis in Section 4.3. This citation must be resolved. The intended reference appears to be: Goh, K.-I., & Barabási, A.-L. (2008). Burstiness and memory in complex systems. *EPL*, 81(4), 48002.

**C5. The manuscript contains blue-colored draft text (Minor).**
Large portions of the methodology, related work, and conclusion are wrapped in `\textcolor{blue}{...}`. This styling should be removed before final submission.

**C6. The table environment is malformed in experiments_results.tex (Minor).**
`\begin{table}` for Table `tab:agent_ranking` contains a nested `\begin{figure}` (the metrics heatmap). This is not valid LaTeX and will cause layout artifacts in the compiled PDF. The figure should be placed outside the table environment.

---

### Minor Observations

**O1. The 40/40/20 query-type split is principled in generation but imprecise in practice.**
Table 1 shows actual case counts of 239 search / 169 multi-hop / 117 report (≈ 45%/32%/22%). The paper correctly notes that "generators produce variable-sized batches." Reporting the actual split rather than only the target split is appropriate and the paper handles this honestly, but a brief explanation of why the target is 40/40/20 (rather than, e.g., an equal 33/33/33) would give the choice more justification than implied.

**O2. Figure quality.**
The manuscript references many PDF figures (pass_rate_by_agent_tenant.pdf, assertion_by_query_type.pdf, etc.) that are generated by a separate Python script. It is assumed these figures are camera-ready, but a final review of font sizes, axis label readability, and color accessibility at the printed figure size is recommended.

**O3. The interactive interface is described but not quantitatively evaluated.**
Section 3.6 describes four navigation views (Chat, Evaluation, Side-by-Side Comparison, Data Generator) and argues that the interface accelerates debugging. No evidence is provided for the "accelerates" claim. For a paper targeting the research community, acknowledging that this is a qualitative rather than quantitative contribution (or including even an informal study) would be more honest.

**O4. Completeness of the comparison table (Table 2).**
The comparison table marks EABench with checkmarks on every row, including "Synthetic data generation," "Configurable agent runtime," "Assertion-based fact checking," "User-level access control," "Interactive debugging," "Pluggable LLM providers," and "Sandboxed tool execution." While this is accurate, such tables are prone to the bias of being constructed post-hoc around the paper's contributions. A brief note on which features were explicitly requested by related-work authors versus which were identified by the EABench team as desiderata would increase the credibility of Table 2.

**O5. The Researcher agent's 65.2s average latency is notable.**
At 65 seconds per query, the Researcher agent is arguably impractical for interactive enterprise use. The paper correctly identifies this as a practical trade-off, but practitioners considering deployment of a Plan-and-Execute architecture would benefit from additional analysis of where the latency is incurred (planning phase vs. execution phase vs. judge evaluation).

---

### Recommendations

The paper makes a solid and useful contribution to enterprise agent evaluation infrastructure. Compared to Reviews 1 and 2, my concerns are lower in severity because the platform design itself is sound and the empirical findings are interpretable even with their limitations acknowledged. The following changes are required or strongly recommended:

1. **(Required)** Fix the missing `goh2008burstiness` citation.
2. **(Required)** Remove `\textcolor{blue}{...}` artifacts from all manuscript text.
3. **(Required)** Fix the malformed nested `figure` inside `table` in experiments_results.tex.
4. **(Strongly recommended)** Add confidence intervals to the main results table and significance tests to key claims.
5. **(Strongly recommended)** Add a brief human-validation component for assertions (even 50 annotated cases per query type would suffice).
6. **(Optional)** Add one controlled ablation isolating the architecture variable.

The paper is closer to the threshold for acceptance than the other reviews suggest, primarily because the platform framing (rather than a pure empirical paper) reduces the bar for statistical rigor and requires a practical engineering contribution, which EABench delivers.
